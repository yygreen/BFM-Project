There are twenty-one programs on this site and only three expiry rules between
them. Six programs never expire miles at all. Nine put a clock on the account
that any activity resets. Six expire miles on a fixed date, and nothing you do
in the account moves it.

Knowing which of the three you are holding is the whole thing. People do not
lose miles because a rule is harsh. They lose them because they assumed their
program worked like the last one they used.

## Group one: never expires, but the account still can

Delta, United, Southwest and JetBlue mean it literally. A balance sits there
forever with no activity of any kind, and there is nothing to manage.

Alaska is the one worth reading twice. Alaska removed mileage expiry in 2023
and kept the policy through the rebrand to Atmos Rewards, so the points have no
clock. The **account** does. Twenty-four months with no earning or redeeming
and it can be frozen for security, which a phone call undoes. Leave it three
years in total and the account can be closed with the balance inside it.

That applies to former HawaiianMiles balances too, because
[since 1 October 2025 they are the same program](/blog/hawaiianmiles-are-now-atmos-rewards).
If you converted a Hawaiian balance and have not touched it since, that is
exactly the account this rule was written for.

## Group two: the clock resets, and it is nearly free

Nine programs expire miles only after a period with nothing happening on the
account. Every one of them resets in full on a single qualifying transaction,
of any size. One mile earned from a shopping portal saves a balance of 200,000.

Two details separate them, and both catch people:

**Redeeming does not always count.** LifeMiles, Frontier Miles and Flying Blue
extend on an **earn**, and spending miles leaves the expiry date exactly where
it was. Burning 5,000 miles to save the rest is a reasonable move in Avios or
Asia Miles and does nothing at all in LifeMiles.

**Etihad narrowed what counts to flying.** Etihad Guest miles expire after 18
months without a qualifying flight on Etihad or a partner airline. Credit card
transfers, shopping partners and mileage purchases no longer extend them. It is
the strictest activity rule in the table and the one most likely to be
remembered wrongly, because it used to work like everyone else's.

**Aeroplan's clock is paused, and the restart has teeth.** Air Canada suspended
points expiry through 29 November 2026. On 30 November the 18-month inactivity
rule comes back, and it looks backward: an account with no activity in the 18
months before that date loses its balance when the rule resumes, rather than
getting a fresh clock. Any single earn or redemption before then protects
everything. If you hold a dormant Aeroplan balance, this is the one date in
this article worth putting in a calendar.

The two Avios programs sit at the generous end at 36 months, which turns out to
be its own trap. Three years is long enough to forget an account exists. We
wrote up [how the Avios clock works and the fastest ways to reset it](/blog/do-avios-expire)
separately.

## Group three: fixed date, no reset

ANA, EVA Air, KrisFlyer, Miles & More, Turkish and Emirates expire miles on a
schedule set when the miles land. Flying, earning and redeeming change nothing.
Miles earned in March 2024 die in March 2027 whatever else the account does.

The mechanics differ in ways worth knowing:

- **ANA and KrisFlyer** count to the end of the 36th month after the earning
  month, so everything earned in a month dies together on the last day of a
  month three years out.
- **EVA Air** counts 36 months from the travel date, or from the date a partner
  transfer was applied. It is the one program in this group with a free
  extension built in: redeem any award miles and fly EVA or UNI Air
  internationally in the final twelve months, and unexpired miles extend 36
  months from that travel date.
- **Miles & More** rounds to the end of the quarter, which usually buys a few
  extra weeks.
- **Turkish** counts calendar years, not rolling months. Miles earned in
  February 2024 and in November 2024 both die on 31 December 2027, so miles
  earned late in a year are worth noticeably less time than miles earned early.
- **Emirates** counts three years to the end of your birthday month, which is
  the only expiry date in the table that depends on who you are rather than
  when you earned.

Three of the six sell you time back. KrisFlyer renews miles for six months at a
published fee, Turkish offers a paid extension, and Emirates reinstates expired
miles for a fee within limits. Status and co-brand cards exempt you in several
of them. None of that is free, and none of it is something to rely on.

## Does buying miles reset the clock?

This is the question we actually get asked, so it is worth answering plainly.

**In the activity-based programs, a mileage purchase normally posts as an earn
and resets the clock like any other.** That is a real side effect worth knowing
about, though it is a poor reason on its own to buy: the cheapest reset is a
few miles from a shopping portal, not a purchase.

**Etihad Guest is the documented exception.** Buying miles does not extend
Etihad Guest miles any more. Only flying does.

**In the fixed-date programs, buying extends nothing.** Miles you buy arrive
with their own clock running from the date they land, which for most of that
group means 36 months, and the miles already in the account keep the dates they
already had. Buying into KrisFlyer in August does not save the KrisFlyer miles
that expire in September.

## What this means if you are about to buy

The rule we work to, and the one we will tell you on the phone:

1. **Buy close to the redemption.** Miles bought for a trip you are booking
   this month have no expiry problem in any of the twenty-one programs.
   Speculative balances are where expiry bites.
2. **Check the balance you already hold before topping it up.** If the account
   is in group three, find out what dies and when. Topping up an account that
   is about to lose 60,000 miles is a bad trade you can avoid by ordering after
   the expiry date rather than before it.
3. **Confirm the program you are actually in.** The Hawaiian and Alaska merger
   caught people out for a year, and Qatar's QMiles became Avios. A rule you
   looked up for a program that no longer exists is not a rule.
